How it works

From question to conversation in three steps.

Choose the avatar you want at the table, interact the way you actually think, and leave with a real conversation grounded in real source material — not another vague chatbot answer.

1

Choose your avatar

A historical figure from the public expert library — Einstein, Caesar, Cleopatra, Confucius, Alexander, da Vinci — or a custom professor avatar built from your own course material.

2

Talk, text, or check in

Type a quick question, hold a real voice conversation, or send a 30-second video check-in. The avatar adapts to your context instead of giving generic advice.

3

Leave with something useful

A clearer idea, a sharper decision, a deeper grasp of the material. Conversations export as memos. For teachers, sessions roll up into a class-wide engagement summary.

Anti-hallucination layer

Built to reduce hallucinations.

For education, the most important feature is not sounding confident. It is knowing when the system is grounded and when it is not.

Source-first

Answer from real material.

Professor avatars answer from uploaded curriculum, slides, readings, transcripts, and approved sources first.

Bounded

Bounded knowledge.

Teachers can restrict an avatar to class material, approved public context, or a specific unit.

Labels

Source labels.

The interface shows whether an answer is grounded in uploaded class material, public-source context, or general reasoning.

Refuse

Refuse when uncertain.

If the answer is outside the available source material, the avatar should say so instead of inventing.

No fakes

No fake citations.

The system must never display citations or source references it did not actually retrieve.

Teacher controls

Teacher-owned boundaries.

Avatar realism

Realism breaks at the mouth.

For a 3D professor avatar, the most important realism layer is not just the face model. It is the timing between speech, mouth movement, expression, pauses, and eye contact. If the mouth feels wrong, the whole experience feels fake.

Speech-synced

Speech-synced mouth movement.

The avatar mouth should move from the actual spoken audio, not from a generic talking animation.

Phoneme

Phoneme-aware lip sync.

The system should map speech sounds to mouth shapes so words feel physically believable.

Pauses

Micro-pauses and hesitation.

A good professor avatar should pause before hard questions, slow down during explanation, and leave room for the student to think.

Expression

Expression timing.

Eyebrows, head movement, eye contact, and smile timing should support the teaching moment.

Latency

Low-latency response loop.

Students should not feel like they are talking to a delayed video. The response loop should feel alive.

Fallback

Fallback realism.

If full lip sync is not available, the system should degrade gracefully instead of showing uncanny mouth movement.

  1. Text response
  2. Voice generation
  3. Audio timing
  4. Phoneme / viseme mapping
  5. Mouth movement
  6. Facial expression
  7. Eye contact + head motion
  8. Student interruption handling
Professor upload trust

Professors should know exactly what the avatar is allowed to say.

A professor avatar is only useful if the professor can trust it. Dinner With Anyone should make uploaded materials, source boundaries, difficulty level, and answer behavior visible and controllable.

If the source supports it, the avatar answers.

If the source does not support it, the avatar says so.

If the professor restricts it, the avatar respects the boundary.
